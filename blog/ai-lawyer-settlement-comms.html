4 AI Prompts for Settlement Negotiations and Client Status Updates

July 30, 2026 7 min read Solo and Small-Firm Attorneys

Settlement correspondence is one of the higher-stakes writing tasks in any litigation or dispute practice — and one of the most formulaic. The structure is consistent: state the facts, make the demand, set a deadline. AI handles the scaffolding well; the attorney provides the judgment and verifies the law.

These four prompts cover the main phases of settlement communication: the initial demand, the counter-response, the client update, and the summary memo documenting what was agreed. Each is designed to produce a draft an attorney reviews and takes ownership of before it goes out.

Ethics framing: Every prompt below produces a draft for attorney review, not a finished document. Settlement letters carry real legal and strategic weight — the attorney signs off on every version before it is sent. These prompts are practice aids, not practice replacements.

Prompt 1 — Settlement Demand Letter

The opening demand letter sets the tone for the entire negotiation. It needs to be firm without being inflammatory, factually grounded, and clear about the demand and timeline.

5 minutes
Copy and paste this prompt
Act as a litigation drafting assistant. Draft a settlement demand letter. Facts of the dispute: [DESCRIBE THE CORE FACTS — who is the claimant, who is the respondent, what happened, what harm resulted]. Legal basis for the claim: [DESCRIBE IN GENERAL TERMS — breach of contract / negligence / personal injury / employment dispute / other; I will verify the specific statutory or case law basis myself]. Damages sought: [$AMOUNT — itemize if helpful: medical expenses $X, lost wages $X, pain and suffering $X, other $X]. Demand: [DESCRIBE WHAT IS BEING REQUESTED — payment, action, or both]. Response deadline: [DATE]. Include: (1) a formal opening establishing representation, (2) a neutral factual summary of the dispute using only the facts I provided — no speculation, (3) a clear statement of the legal basis in general terms, (4) the specific demand and deadline, (5) a professional closing noting that failure to respond may result in further legal action without specifying exactly what that is. Flag any place where you guessed or filled a gap I didn't provide. Under 400 words.

After generating: verify all cited facts against your file, confirm the legal theory with your actual research, and adjust the tone as needed for the specific opposing party and your negotiating strategy.

Prompt 2 — Response to a Settlement Counter-Offer

When opposing counsel counters, you need to respond quickly and strategically. This prompt structures a written response that either accepts a counter, rejects it with a revised position, or holds firm at the original demand.

4 minutes
Copy and paste this prompt
Draft a response to a settlement counter-offer from opposing counsel. Our original demand: [$AMOUNT or describe terms]. Their counter-offer: [$AMOUNT or describe their proposed terms]. Our response (choose one and delete the others): [A — we accept their counter-offer / B — we reject and counter at $AMOUNT with the following terms: describe / C — we reject the counter and hold firm at our original demand]. If B: briefly state the factual or legal basis for maintaining a higher position (I will verify the substance). If C: briefly state why the counter is insufficient without over-disclosing litigation strategy. In all cases: (1) maintain a professional and non-inflammatory tone with opposing counsel, (2) be clear about what we are proposing or holding, (3) include a new response deadline [DATE]. Under 200 words. Flag any place where I left a bracket unfilled.

Keep counter-offer responses shorter than the original demand letter. Length in negotiation correspondence can read as desperation or confusion. Be clear; be brief; set the new deadline.

Prompt 3 — Client Status Update on Settlement Progress

Clients hate radio silence during litigation. This prompt writes a status update that keeps them informed without making promises, disclosing strategy, or raising expectations you cannot deliver on.

3 minutes
Copy and paste this prompt
Write a client status update email on settlement negotiations. Client name: [NAME]. Matter: [BRIEF DESCRIPTION]. Current status of negotiations: [DESCRIBE — e.g., demand sent on DATE, counter-offer received, we are responding, talks ongoing]. What has happened since the last update: [DESCRIBE — or write "no significant change" if there is no news]. Next step: [DESCRIBE — e.g., we are reviewing their counter / we expect a response by DATE / we are scheduling a call to discuss]. Client action required: [YES — describe / NO]. Requirements: (1) do not speculate about likely outcome or timeline unless you have told me to, (2) do not disclose negotiating strategy or positions beyond what is stated above, (3) keep the tone professional and reassuring without making promises, (4) include a clear invitation to call or email with questions. Under 150 words.

Send this proactively — do not wait until the client calls asking for an update. Regular short updates, even when nothing has changed, dramatically reduce client anxiety and the volume of status calls your office fields.

Prompt 4 — Settlement Summary Memorandum

Once a settlement is reached, document it clearly and immediately — in writing, before anyone starts misremembering what was agreed. This prompt produces the internal memo and the client-facing summary.

5 minutes
Copy and paste this prompt
Write a settlement summary memorandum documenting a resolved matter. Matter: [DESCRIPTION]. Parties: [Claimant name(s)] vs. [Respondent name(s)]. Settlement terms agreed: [LIST ALL TERMS — payment amount, payment schedule, confidentiality clause if any, release scope, any non-monetary terms, who pays costs]. Effective date: [DATE settlement was agreed]. Pending steps to finalize: [e.g., formal agreement to be drafted and signed by DATE / payment due by DATE / court dismissal to be filed]. Write two versions: (1) a one-page internal memorandum for the file, third-person, formal legal style; (2) a 3–4 sentence plain-language summary for the client explaining what was agreed and what happens next. In both versions, include only the terms I have provided — do not infer or add standard boilerplate terms I did not list. Flag any bracket left unfilled.

The internal memo and the client summary serve different readers — the internal version is for your file and future reference; the client summary is for their understanding. Keeping them separate avoids the common problem of sending a client a document that reads like it was written for a paralegal.
