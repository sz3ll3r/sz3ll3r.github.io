5 AI Prompts Every Solo Attorney Should Use Weekly

These five prompts handle the most time-consuming routine drafting tasks in a small firm — tested with Claude.ai and ChatGPT, ready to paste.

June 29, 2026 · 8 min read · By Shane Zeller

The promise of AI in law is not replacing attorneys — it is eliminating the mechanical drafting that consumes 30–40% of a solo practitioner's week. Engagement letters, demand letters, client status updates, contract summary memos, follow-up emails after consultations: none of this requires judgment. All of it eats time.

These five prompts cover the highest-frequency drafting tasks in a solo or small-firm practice. Each is designed so you paste it in, fill in the bracketed fields with your case facts, and get a first draft in under two minutes. You edit, you verify, you send. The AI handles the mechanical starting point.

Important: These prompts require attorney review

AI output is a draft, not a final product. Every document produced with these prompts must be reviewed, edited, and verified by a licensed attorney. Do not paste client-identifying information into free AI tiers — use placeholder names and substitute real details only in reviewed output.

Prompt 1 — Engagement Letter

Engagement letters take 15–25 minutes to draft from scratch. Most of the language is identical across matters. This prompt generates a clean, professional engagement letter in about 90 seconds.

You are a legal document assistant helping a solo [PRACTICE AREA] attorney draft a client engagement letter. Client name: [CLIENT NAME] Matter: [BRIEF DESCRIPTION — e.g., "personal injury auto accident, defendant TBD"] Fee structure: [HOURLY at $X/hr | FLAT FEE of $X | CONTINGENCY at X%] Retainer required: [YES, $X | NO] Jurisdiction: [STATE] Draft a professional engagement letter that covers: 1. Scope of representation (specific to this matter only) 2. Fee structure and payment terms as described above 3. Client responsibilities (providing documents, responding promptly) 4. How the attorney-client relationship ends 5. Conflict of interest acknowledgment 6. Electronic communication consent Use a professional but accessible tone. Keep it under 600 words. Do not make jurisdiction-specific legal representations — note that the client should review terms with any questions.
Time saved: 15–20 minutes per matter

Prompt 2 — Demand Letter (Breach of Contract)

Demand letters have a predictable structure: establish the contract, document the breach, quantify damages, state the demand, set a deadline. This prompt produces a firm, professional first draft that you adjust for tone and add citations.

You are a legal writing assistant helping draft a demand letter for breach of contract. Representing: [PLAINTIFF NAME] Opposing party: [DEFENDANT NAME / COMPANY] Contract type: [e.g., service agreement, construction contract, employment agreement] Contract date: [DATE] What defendant agreed to do: [BRIEF DESCRIPTION] What defendant failed to do: [SPECIFIC BREACH] Damages: [AMOUNT AND BASIS — e.g., "$12,500 in unpaid invoices" or "$8,000 repair costs caused by defective work"] Response deadline: [NUMBER] days Draft a professional demand letter that: - States the facts of the agreement and breach clearly and without inflammatory language - Quantifies damages with specificity - Demands payment or performance by the deadline - Notes that legal action will follow if demand is not met - Avoids threats that could constitute extortion under [STATE] law Tone: firm and professional. Length: 400–500 words.
Time saved: 20–30 minutes per demand letter

Prompt 3 — Client Status Update Email

Status update emails are the highest-volume communication task for most small firms. Clients ask for them constantly. This prompt handles the standard update in under a minute.

You are a professional legal assistant drafting a client status update email for a [PRACTICE AREA] attorney. Client name: [CLIENT NAME] (use first name in the email) Matter: [BRIEF DESCRIPTION] Current status: [WHERE THINGS STAND — e.g., "We have filed the complaint and are waiting for defendant to respond. Deadline for response is [DATE]."] Next step: [WHAT HAPPENS NEXT and approximate timeline] Any action needed from client: [YES — describe | NO] Write a clear, warm status update email that: - Opens by acknowledging the client's patience - Explains current status in plain English (no jargon) - States next steps clearly with timing - Notes any action required from the client - Closes with an invitation to call with questions Length: 150–200 words. Tone: professional but warm and human.
Time saved: 8–12 minutes per status email

Prompt 4 — Contract Review Summary Memo

When a client brings you a contract to review, the first deliverable is usually a memo summarizing the key terms and flagging risk areas. This prompt generates the skeleton of that memo — you fill in the analysis.

You are a legal writing assistant helping draft a contract review summary memo. Contract type: [e.g., commercial lease, vendor services agreement, employment offer letter] Client role: [BUYER/TENANT/EMPLOYEE/SERVICE PROVIDER] Key terms you have identified: [LIST KEY TERMS — e.g., "5-year term, $4,200/month, no assignment without landlord consent, 30-day cure period for default"] Significant risk areas: [LIST RISKS — e.g., "unlimited personal guarantee, no cap on liability, automatic renewal with 90-day opt-out window"] Draft a client-facing memo that: 1. Summarizes the contract's key business terms in plain English (not legal jargon) 2. Highlights the top 3–5 risk areas the client should understand before signing 3. Notes 2–3 terms the client may want to negotiate 4. Includes a "Questions for Counterparty" section with 3 suggested questions Format with clear headings. Length: 400–600 words. Assume the client is a business owner, not an attorney.
Time saved: 30–45 minutes per contract review memo

Prompt 5 — Post-Consultation Follow-Up

The follow-up email after an initial consultation is one of the highest-leverage pieces of communication in client development. A good one converts prospects. Most attorneys never send one, or send a generic one-liner. This prompt produces a memorable, specific follow-up.

You are a professional legal assistant drafting a post-consultation follow-up email for a solo [PRACTICE AREA] attorney. Prospect name: [NAME] Consultation date: [DATE] Their situation: [2–3 sentence summary of their legal issue] What you discussed: [KEY POINTS — e.g., "options available, timeline, fee structure you presented"] Next step you proposed: [e.g., "they will send documents by Friday; you will call Monday to discuss retainer"] Write a post-consultation follow-up email that: - Thanks them specifically for their time (mention a specific detail from the conversation) - Summarizes the 2–3 key takeaways from the consultation (shows you listened) - Restates the next step clearly - Includes a soft, non-pushy statement about being ready to help if they decide to move forward - Closes warmly Length: 200–250 words. Tone: personal, warm, professional. This is a relationship email, not a sales pitch.
Time saved: 15–20 minutes per follow-up; measurable impact on conversion

How to use these prompts effectively

A few practices that make the difference between useful output and output you end up rewriting entirely:

Be specific with the fill-in fields. Vague inputs produce vague drafts. "Contract dispute" gives you a generic template. "5-year commercial lease, tenant defaulted on month 18 of 60, landlord seeking unpaid rent plus acceleration clause" gives you something you can actually use.

Use placeholder names for confidential matters. On free AI tiers, use "Client A" and "Opposing Party B" rather than real names. Substitute the real names after you have reviewed and edited the draft in your own system.

Always review before sending. These prompts produce first drafts, not final work product. Check for accuracy, verify any legal standards mentioned, and adjust to match your voice. The goal is eliminating the blank-page problem — not eliminating your judgment.

Build a prompt library. Copy the prompts that work for your practice areas into a notes app, Notion, or Word document. Customize the defaults (jurisdiction, your typical fee structure, your tone preferences) so your starting point is already half-personalized next time.
